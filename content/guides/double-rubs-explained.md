"Double rubs" is the number you'll see on almost every upholstery fabric spec sheet, and it's the quickest way to tell whether a fabric will survive the job you have in mind. Here's what it means, how many you need, and what the number doesn't tell you.

## What a double rub is

In the **Wyzenbeek test** (the standard used in North America), a sample of fabric is pulled tight and a piece of cotton duck or wire screen is rubbed back and forth across it. One forward-and-back pass is one **double rub**. The test runs until the fabric shows noticeable wear or breaks, and the result is the number of double rubs it survived.

So a fabric rated at 50,000 double rubs withstood 50,000 back-and-forth passes before wearing through. Many fabrics are simply listed as "exceeds 100,000 double rubs", because testing usually stops there.

## How many double rubs do you need?

These are common guidelines for **Wyzenbeek double rubs**. For a busy home, aim one level up.

| Use | Double rubs | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Decorative | Under 10,000 | Pillows, headboards, drapery, occasional chairs |
| Light home use | 10,000 to 15,000 | Accent chairs, bedroom seating, rarely used furniture |
| Everyday home use | 15,000 to 30,000 | Living room sofas and chairs, ottomans |
| Heavy home use | 30,000 to 50,000 | Family sofas, dining chairs, homes with kids or pets |
| Commercial | 30,000 and up | Offices, restaurants, hotels, waiting rooms |
| Heavy-duty commercial | 100,000 and up | Restaurant booths, transit, healthcare, high traffic |

## Wyzenbeek vs. Martindale

Europe and much of the world use the **Martindale** test instead. There the fabric is rubbed in a figure-eight pattern against worsted wool, and the result is counted in **cycles**. Very roughly, 20,000 to 25,000 Martindale cycles suits general home use and 30,000 or more suits heavy home or commercial use.

The two tests rub the fabric in different ways, so **there's no reliable conversion between them**. Compare Wyzenbeek numbers with Wyzenbeek numbers, and Martindale with Martindale.

## What double rubs don't tell you

A high number means the fabric resists abrasion. It doesn't guarantee everything else:

- **Pilling.** Some fabrics, especially soft, fuzzy ones, can form little balls on the surface even with a high rating.
- **Snagging.** Loose weaves and loops (like bouclé) can catch on claws and jewellery regardless of rubs.
- **Fading.** Sun resistance is tested separately (lightfastness). Look for "fade resistant" or solution-dyed fibres for sunny rooms.
- **Stains.** Cleanability depends on the fibre and finish. Check the [cleaning code](/guides/upholstery-cleaning-codes).
- **Seam slippage and construction.** How the piece is upholstered matters as much as the fabric.

## How to use the rating when you shop

1. Decide how hard the piece will work: a formal chair, a family sofa, or a restaurant booth.
2. Pick a minimum from the table above, then check the **Durability** line in each fabric's specifications.
3. For homes with pets or kids, also look at the weave (tight is better) and the cleaning code.
4. Order a [free sample](/sample-books) and feel it: a tightly woven fabric with a firm hand usually wears best.

## Common questions

### Is 15,000 double rubs good for a sofa?

It's acceptable for a sofa that gets light use. For a sofa used every day, look for 30,000 or more, and for a family sofa with kids or pets, higher still.

### Is 100,000 double rubs worth it for home furniture?

You won't wear it out, but the rating alone doesn't make a fabric pet-proof or stain-proof. Choose it for durability, then check the weave and cleaning code for everything else.

### Can I convert Martindale to double rubs?

Not reliably. The tests use different motions and abrasives, so compare fabrics using the same test.
