Every fabric spec sheet lists a **pattern direction**: either *railroaded* or *up the roll* (also called *not railroaded*). It sounds technical, but it decides where seams go on your sofa and how much fabric you need.

## The two directions

Upholstery fabric comes off the roll 54 inches wide and as long as you order.

- **Up the roll (not railroaded):** the pattern runs **along the length** of the roll, the way you'd read it if you unrolled the fabric away from you. The fabric's 54-inch width becomes the width of each panel on the furniture.
- **Railroaded:** the pattern is turned **sideways**, running across the width. The fabric is used turned on its side, so the length of the roll becomes the width across the furniture.

For plain fabrics and textures with no direction, it matters less. For stripes, florals and anything with a clear top and bottom, it decides how the pattern sits on the furniture.

## Why it matters

### Seams on long pieces

A three-seat sofa back or a long bench cushion is often wider than 54 inches.

- With **up-the-roll** fabric, that piece needs two widths seamed together, which adds a seam and often more fabric.
- With **railroaded** fabric, the piece can be cut in one continuous length, with **no seam** across the back or seat.

That's why railroaded fabrics are popular for sofas, banquettes, headboards and long benches.

### Stripes

The direction decides which way a stripe ends up on the furniture. Used up the roll, a stripe that runs along the roll sits vertically on a chair back; used railroaded, it runs horizontally. The spec sheet tells you how the photo is shown ("shown railroaded" or "shown up the roll"), so you can picture it on the piece.

### Yardage

Railroading can **save fabric** on long, low pieces, while up-the-roll fabric can be more efficient for tall, narrow pieces like chair backs. Your upholsterer will lay out the pieces to suit the direction. See [how much fabric do I need?](/guides/how-much-fabric-do-i-need)

## Which should you choose?

| Piece | Usually best |
|---|---|
| Long sofa back or seat | Railroaded (no seams) |
| Long bench cushion or banquette | Railroaded |
| Wide headboard | Railroaded |
| Dining chairs, accent chairs | Either |
| Curtains | Up the roll (pattern runs top to bottom) |
| Stripes | Ask your upholsterer: it depends on which way the stripe runs on the roll |

> You'll find the direction under **Pattern direction** in the specifications on every fabric page in our shop.

## Can you railroad any fabric?

Plain fabrics and textures with no direction can usually be used either way, and your upholsterer can decide. A fabric with a one-way pattern, a pile (like velvet) or a visible weave direction should be used as the mill intended, because turning it changes how it looks and catches the light.

## Common questions

### What does "shown railroaded" mean?

It means the photo shows the fabric in the direction it's meant to be used on the furniture: with the pattern running across the roll.

### Is railroaded fabric better for sofas?

Often, yes. It lets long pieces like the sofa back and seat be cut without seams. Plain fabrics can usually be railroaded by your upholsterer either way.

### Does railroaded fabric need more yardage?

Usually not. On long, low pieces it often needs less, because there are no extra widths to seam together.
